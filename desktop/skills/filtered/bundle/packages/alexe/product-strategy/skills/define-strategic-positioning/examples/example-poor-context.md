# Example: Poor Context

## Input

"Help us define our positioning."

## Output

### Status
Insufficient context to define a strategic positioning.

### What is missing

Strategic positioning requires making deliberate choices about who to serve, what category to compete in, and why the product is the right choice. These choices cannot be made without:

1. **Target segment** — who is this product for, and who is it not for?
2. **Product capabilities** — what does the product actually do well?
3. **Business goals** — what does winning look like for this organization?

### Discovery questions

Before defining positioning, answer:

1. Who are the primary users or customers? (Industry, role, company size, use case)
2. What does the product do — what job does it perform for users?
3. Who are the 2–3 closest competitors or alternatives users consider?
4. Why do current customers choose this product over alternatives? (If known)
5. What is the business goal — growth, retention, moving upmarket, entering a new segment?

### What happens next

Once segment, capabilities, and business goals are available, this skill can produce:
- A target segment definition (with explicit exclusions)
- Frame of reference and point of differentiation
- A positioning statement
- An assessment of positioning trade-offs

If win/loss data or customer interviews are available, include them — they are the most reliable input for positioning decisions.
