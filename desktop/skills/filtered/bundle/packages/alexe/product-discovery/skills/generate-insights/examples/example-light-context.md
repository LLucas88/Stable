## Input

Product: Listo — a B2C grocery delivery app operating in three cities.

Context: "Our interviews suggested users care most about delivery speed, and a few mentioned being frustrated when items are out of stock. We had about 5 interviews but didn't take detailed notes. We're working from memory."

## Expected output

Insight 1 (provisional): Delivery speed may be a hygiene factor rather than a differentiator. Users likely expect fast delivery as a baseline and penalise slowness, but faster delivery alone may not drive preference or retention.
- Supporting evidence: Team recall of "caring about delivery speed" across approximately 5 interviews.
- Evidence quality: Single data point — memory-based recall with no structured notes. Cannot verify whether users described speed as a reason they chose Listo, a reason they stayed, or a reason they would leave. The distinction matters.
- Confidence: Low.

Insight 2 (provisional): Out-of-stock frustration may be about broken expectations rather than missing items. If users built a cart and then discovered substitutions or removals at checkout or delivery, the frustration is likely about wasted effort and unpredictability rather than the specific missing product.
- Supporting evidence: Implied from team recall of "frustration when items are out of stock," but the exact user language and the moment of frustration (during browsing, at checkout, at delivery) are not documented.
- Evidence quality: Single data point — inferred, not directly observed.
- Confidence: Low.

Contradictory or complicating data: Unknown — without notes, contradictions cannot be identified.

What changes relative to prior assumptions: Unknown — without documented prior assumptions, cannot compare.

Gaps: Almost everything that would be needed to act on these insights:
- No transcripts or structured notes — recall bias is likely and unquantifiable
- Frequency of themes cannot be assessed
- No distinction between participants — cannot tell if speed and stock issues came from the same user or different ones
- Cannot separate observations (what users said) from interpretations (what the team inferred)
- No data on competitive context — are users comparing Listo to another delivery app, to supermarket trips, or to something else?

Note: These are candidate insights only — they should be treated as hypotheses to validate in the next research round, not findings to act on.

Recommended next step: Re-run 5–8 interviews with structured note-taking. Probe specifically on what triggers an order (planned vs. spontaneous), what happens when an item is unavailable (at what point do they discover it), and what "fast enough" means in their context.
